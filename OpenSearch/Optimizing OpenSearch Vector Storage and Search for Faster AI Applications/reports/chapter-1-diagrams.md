# Chapter 1 diagram recommendations

This report lists eight places in the Chapter 1 README where a diagram or chart would genuinely help students hold a spatial or numeric relationship in their heads, with a paste-ready image prompt for each.

## 1. From raw data to a point in vector space

**Placement:** Lesson 1-1, before Step 1. Insert it directly after the vectors-and-embeddings concept paragraph, which ends with "OpenSearch stores embeddings in a `knn_vector` field and searches them by distance."

**Type:** Labeled illustration.

**Why:** Students meet the abstract idea "a book description becomes 768 floats" before they run any command, and a picture of data flowing into a shared space where distance means similarity anchors everything that follows.

**Creation prompt:** Create a horizontal three-stage labeled illustration titled "From data to vectors". Stage 1, on the left, shows three small document cards stacked vertically. Card 1 says "Book description: 'A thrilling space adventure'". Card 2 says "Book description: 'A galactic war epic'". Card 3 says "Book description: 'A cozy baking cookbook'". Stage 2, in the middle, shows one rounded box labeled "Embedding model". Three arrows go from the three cards into this box. One caption under the box reads "Turns each text into a list of numbers (for example 768 floats)". Stage 3, on the right, shows a simple 2D coordinate plane labeled "Vector space (hundreds of dimensions, drawn here as 2)". Plot three dots on the plane. The two space books sit close together in the upper right and share one dashed circle labeled "Similar meaning = close together". The cookbook dot sits alone in the lower left, far from the circle. Three arrows go from the embedding model box to the three dots. Add one small footnote at the bottom: "A naive color embedding could use just 3 dimensions: red, green, blue. Real models use hundreds to capture nuance." Arrange everything left to right. Style: clean flat technical illustration for a video course slide, white background, limited color palette (blues and grays with one accent color), large readable labels, no logos, no watermark.

## 2. Inside the index: nodes, shards, and segments, before and after force-merge

**Placement:** Lesson 1-1, Step 5. Insert it after the opening paragraph that ends "(Only force-merge indexes that are done ingesting; it is expensive.)" and before the first `_cat/shards` request.

**Type:** Architecture diagram with a before and after panel.

**Why:** Students must picture a three-level containment hierarchy (index holds shards, shards hold segments, segments hold documents) to make sense of the `_cat` output and the force-merge result, and text alone makes that nesting easy to scramble.

**Creation prompt:** Create a two-panel architecture diagram titled "One index, two shards, and what force-merge does". The left panel is titled "Before force-merge". It shows a cluster containing two server boxes side by side, labeled "Node 1" and "Node 2". Node 1 contains a box labeled "Shard 0 (Primary)". Node 2 contains a box labeled "Shard 0 (Replica)". A bracket above both nodes is labeled "Index: vector-fundamentals (50 documents)". Inside the primary shard box, draw two smaller stacked boxes labeled "Segment _0 (docs 1-31)" and "Segment _1 (docs 32-50)". Add a small lock icon on each segment with the caption "Segments are immutable Lucene files". Under the left panel add the caption "More segments = higher query latency". The right panel is titled "After POST _forcemerge?max_num_segments=1". It repeats the same two nodes, but the primary shard now contains a single wider box labeled "Segment _2 (all 50 documents)". Under the right panel add the caption "One segment = fewer files to scan per query". A large arrow between the two panels is labeled "_forcemerge (expensive, run only after ingest is done)". Arrange the panels left to right. Style: clean flat technical illustration for a video course slide, white background, limited color palette (blues and grays with one accent color), large readable labels, no logos, no watermark.

## 3. Disk-based storage: the two-phase quantize-then-rescore search

**Placement:** Lesson 1-1, Step 6. Insert it after the opening paragraph that ends "to about 1/16th the size of the full float32 precision." and before the `PUT vector-disk-demo` request.

**Type:** Architecture diagram with a memory layout and a numbered flow.

**Why:** The phrase "compressed form in memory, full fidelity on disk, two-phase search" describes a spatial memory layout plus a sequence, and students cannot verify either from the API response alone.

**Creation prompt:** Create an architecture diagram titled "mode: on_disk, small in RAM, full precision on disk". Draw two large horizontal zones stacked top to bottom. The top zone is labeled "RAM" and contains a row of small thin bars labeled "Compressed vectors (quantized to 1/16th size with compression_level: 16x)". The bottom zone is labeled "Disk" and contains a row of wide bars labeled "Full float32 vectors (full fidelity)". On the left side, draw a query icon labeled "knn query" with two numbered arrows. Arrow 1 points into the RAM zone and is labeled "Phase 1: fast scan of compressed vectors picks candidates". Arrow 2 goes from the RAM zone down into the Disk zone and is labeled "Phase 2: rescore only the candidates with full-precision vectors". A final arrow exits the Disk zone to the right toward a box labeled "Accurate top-k results". Along the bottom, add a small horizontal scale showing the valid compression levels "1x, 2x, 4x, 8x, 16x, 32x" with 16x highlighted by the accent color and a tag reading "used in this lab", and 32x tagged "on_disk default". Add one caption: "Less RAM cost, strong recall preserved by rescoring." Style: clean flat technical illustration for a video course slide, white background, limited color palette (blues and grays with one accent color), large readable labels, no logos, no watermark.

## 4. HNSW layered graph versus IVF centroid buckets

**Placement:** Lesson 1-2, in the kNN-vs-aNN concept section. Insert it after the bullet list that describes HNSW and IVF, directly before the sentence "You'll run all three against the same 10 product vectors and compare."

**Type:** Side-by-side labeled illustration of two data structures.

**Why:** HNSW's layered graph descent and IVF's centroid buckets are the two core spatial structures of this whole chapter, and students who cannot picture them cannot reason about m, ef_search, nlist, or nprobes later.

**Creation prompt:** Create a side-by-side comparison illustration titled "Two ways to search approximately: HNSW vs IVF". The left half is titled "HNSW: layered graph". Draw three stacked horizontal layers of dots connected by thin lines, top to bottom. The top layer is sparse with 3 dots and is labeled "Top layer: few nodes, long hops". The middle layer has about 8 dots and is labeled "Middle layer". The bottom layer is dense with about 15 dots and is labeled "Bottom layer: all vectors". Draw one bold accent-colored path that enters at a top-layer dot labeled "Entry point", hops across the top layer, descends to the middle layer, hops closer, then descends to the bottom layer and ends at a starred dot labeled "Nearest neighbor". Add a caption under the left half: "m: 16 = up to 16 links per node. Very fast, scales well, high memory." The right half is titled "IVF: centroid buckets". Draw one flat 2D region divided into 4 irregular cells, like a Voronoi diagram, with one bold centroid dot in each cell and small vector dots scattered inside every cell. Label the centroids "4 centroids (nlist: 4), learned by k-means training". Place a starred query point near the boundary of two cells. Shade those two nearest cells with the accent color and label them "Only these buckets get scanned (nprobes: 2)". Gray out the other two cells and label them "Skipped". Add a caption under the right half: "Lower memory than HNSW, fast on huge datasets, but requires a training step first." Arrange the two halves left and right with a thin divider. Style: clean flat technical illustration for a video course slide, white background, limited color palette (blues and grays with one accent color), large readable labels, no logos, no watermark.

## 5. The 10 product vectors: three clusters and the query point

The upper left cluster is colored with the accent color and labeled "electronics"; it contains four dots labeled "p1 Wireless Headphones", "p2 Bluetooth Speaker", "p3 Smart Watch", and "p10 Portable Charger". The upper right cluster is a medium blue and labeled "books"; it contains three dots labeled "p4 Hardcover Novel", "p5 Cookbook Deluxe", and "p6 Poetry Collection". The lower middle cluster is gray-blue and labeled "outdoor"; it contains three dots labeled "p7 Camping Tent", "p8 Hiking Backpack", and "p9 Trekking Poles". Add one starred point inside the electronics cluster labeled "Query vector [0.90, 0.90, 0.10, 0.10, 0.05, 0.05, 0.10, 0.10]". Draw a dashed circle around the query star and its three closest dots (p1, p2, p3) labeled "Top 3 nearest neighbors returned by every method in Steps 9-14". Add a footnote: "Electronics vectors are high in dimensions 1-2, books in dimensions 3-4, outdoor in dimensions 5-6." Style: clean flat technical illustration for a video course slide, white background, limited color palette (blues and grays with one accent color), large readable labels, no logos, no watermark.

## 6. The IVF pipeline: train a model, then build the index from it

**Placement:** Lesson 1-2, Step 12. Insert it after the opening paragraph, which ends by explaining that training is asynchronous and returns a `model_id` immediately, and before the train request. It also serves as the roadmap for Step 13.

**Type:** Flowchart.

**Why:** IVF spreads one logical action across two steps and four API calls (train, poll, create index, reindex), and students routinely confuse the trained model with a searchable index until they see the pipeline as one flow.

**Creation prompt:** Create a left-to-right flowchart titled "IVF setup: from training data to a searchable index" with five numbered stages connected by arrows. Stage 1 is a box labeled "Existing index: products-hnsw" with the sub-label "10 product vectors, dimension 8". Stage 2 is a box labeled "POST _plugins/_knn/models/_train" with the sub-label "k-means clusters the vectors into nlist: 4 centroids"; the arrow into it is labeled "reads training_field: product_vector". Stage 3 is a box labeled "Trained model (model_id)" with the sub-label "Stores the 4 centroids plus dimension, engine, and space_type"; add a small circular arrow beside it labeled "Poll GET model until state goes from training to created". Stage 4 is a box labeled "PUT products-ivf" with the sub-label "Mapping references model_id only. No method block, no dimension. The field inherits everything from the model." Stage 5 is a box labeled "POST _reindex" with the sub-label "Copies all 10 docs; each vector is filed into the bucket of its nearest centroid, building the inverted file". After stage 5, draw a final rounded box labeled "Searchable IVF index" with the sub-label "knn query with nprobes controls how many buckets get scanned". Below the whole flow add one warning strip in the accent color: "Centroids freeze at training time. If your data drifts, retrain a new model and reindex." Style: clean flat technical illustration for a video course slide, white background, limited color palette (blues and grays with one accent color), large readable labels, no logos, no watermark.

## 7. Truncation versus chunking for long documents

**Placement:** Lesson 1-3, in the "Chunking" section. Insert it after the "Concept." paragraph that ends "stores each chunk as its own document linked back to the parent." and before the "Benefits:" paragraph.

**Type:** Two-path flowchart.

**Why:** The silent-truncation failure mode is invisible in any API response, so a picture of the bad path next to the good path is the only way students see what chunking actually prevents.

**Creation prompt:** Create a two-path flowchart titled "Why chunking: one long document, two outcomes". At the top center, draw one tall document icon labeled "Long document (3,000 characters)" with a dashed horizontal line across it near the top labeled "Model limit: about 512 tokens, roughly 1,000-1,200 characters". From this document, draw two paths downward. The left path is titled "Without chunking" and uses gray. Arrow 1 goes to a box labeled "Embedding model". Arrow 2 goes to a torn-paper icon labeled "Everything past the limit is silently cut off". Arrow 3 ends at a red-tinted box labeled "One poor embedding, lost context". The right path is titled "With chunking" and uses the accent color. Arrow 1 goes to a splitter box labeled "Split into chunks that each fit the limit", which fans out into three small document icons labeled "Chunk 1", "Chunk 2", and "Chunk 3". Each chunk has its own arrow into a shared box labeled "Embedding model", and each continues to its own small vector icon. The three vector icons land in a container labeled "Index: one document per chunk", and each chunk document shows a small tag reading "parent_id links back to the original". Under the right path add the caption "Every piece fits the model, and smaller chunks give sharper semantic matches". Under the left path add the caption "No error is raised; the embedding is just quietly worse". Arrange top to bottom with the two paths side by side. Style: clean flat technical illustration for a video course slide, white background, limited color palette (blues and grays with one accent color), large readable labels, no logos, no watermark.

## 8. Dimension reduction: 256 floats in, 128 floats out

**Placement:** Lesson 1-3, Step 16. Insert it after the paragraph that begins "Now what happens when we scale that ratio up" and before the footprint-comparison screenshot.

**Type:** Before and after comparison graphic with proportional bars.

**Why:** The payoff of the whole exercise is a numeric ratio (half the floats, roughly half the vector payload, at any scale), and a proportional visual makes the 49kb baseline and the halving stick better than two lines of `_cat` output.

**Creation prompt:** Create a before-and-after comparison graphic titled "Cutting dimensions in half cuts the vector payload in half". The left panel is titled "Before: my-vector-index". It shows a wide horizontal bar labeled "1 vector = 256 floats x 4 bytes = 1,024 bytes", drawn at full width, and below it the stats "20 documents" and "pri.store.size: 49kb (vectors plus HNSW graph plus metadata)". The right panel is titled "After: my-optimized-vector-index". It shows a horizontal bar of the same height but exactly half the width, labeled "1 vector = 128 floats x 4 bytes = 512 bytes", and below it the stats "20 documents" and "pri.store.size: roughly half (not exactly 2x, because titles, metadata, and segment overhead do not shrink)". Between the panels draw one large arrow labeled "POST _reindex with Painless: ctx._source.my_vector = ctx._source.my_vector.subList(0, 128)". Under both panels add one full-width callout strip in the accent color: "The percentage holds at any scale. Over-dimensioned vectors pay this cost on every document, in RAM as well as on disk, because the HNSW graph lives in memory." Add a small footnote: "Naive truncation is only safe for Matryoshka-style embeddings; real pipelines re-embed, apply PCA, or quantize instead. The migration pattern is what transfers." Arrange the panels left to right. Style: clean flat technical illustration for a video course slide, white background, limited color palette (blues and grays with one accent color), large readable labels, no logos, no watermark.
