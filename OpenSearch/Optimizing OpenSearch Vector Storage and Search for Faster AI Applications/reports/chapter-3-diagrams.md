# Chapter 3 diagram recommendations

This report identifies eight places in the Chapter 3 README where a diagram or chart would carry a concept that students currently must assemble in their heads, and each entry includes a self-contained prompt you can paste into ChatGPT.

## 1. Lexical vs sparse vs dense: three ways to represent the same text

**Placement:** Lesson 3-1, Step 2, immediately after the intro paragraph that begins "Time to meet the other kind of embedding model" and before the line "Replace `YOUR_MODEL_GROUP_ID`".

**Type:** Three-panel comparison graphic.

**Why:** Students must hold three different data representations in their heads for the rest of the chapter, and a side-by-side picture anchors the difference between term matching, a token-to-weight map, and a fixed float array before any of them is queried.

**Creation prompt:** Create a three-panel side-by-side comparison graphic titled "Three ways to represent the same query". All three panels process the same input text, shown once at the top center in a rounded box: "a hero on a dangerous sea voyage". Draw one arrow from this box down into each of the three panels. Panel 1 (left) is titled "Lexical (BM25)" with subtitle "exact term matching". Inside it show the query split into literal tokens as small chips: "hero", "dangerous", "sea", "voyage", and a note underneath: "Matches only these exact tokens in the inverted index. 'voyages' does not match 'voyage'. No model involved." Panel 2 (center) is titled "Sparse embedding" with subtitle "model: opensearch-neural-sparse-encoding-v1". Inside it show a variable-length map of token keys to weights, rendered as a short vertical list with bars proportional to the weight: "sea: 1.9", "voyage: 1.7", "ship: 1.1", "journey: 0.9", "ocean: 0.8", "hero: 0.7", "danger: 0.6". Mark "ship", "journey", and "ocean" with a small tag reading "expansion term (not in the query)". Add a note underneath: "Illustrative weights. Variable length, string keys, only meaningful tokens kept. Stored in an ordinary inverted index." Panel 3 (right) is titled "Dense embedding" with subtitle "model: msmarco-distilbert-base-tas-b". Inside it show a fixed-length array rendered as a long horizontal strip of small cells with sample values "0.021, -0.114, 0.087, … , 0.043" and a bracket label "768 floats, every position filled". Add a note underneath: "Fixed length, meaning spread across all dimensions, no readable tokens. Stored in a k-NN structure." At the bottom, one caption line spanning all panels: "Same query, three data shapes. Chapter 3 fuses the first two; Step 9 compares all three." Layout: three equal panels left to right, input box centered above them. Style: clean flat technical illustration for a video course slide, white background, limited color palette (blues and grays with one accent color), large readable labels, no logos, no watermark.

## 2. The two-processor ingest pipeline with chunk overlap

**Placement:** Lesson 3-1, Step 4, immediately after the four explanatory bullets (the list that ends with "drops tokens weighted below 10% of the max to keep vectors small") and before the line "Replace `YOUR_SPARSE_MODEL_ID`".

**Type:** Left-to-right flowchart with an inset overlap illustration.

**Why:** Students must picture two processors running in sequence and one document fanning out into several overlapping chunks, and the text describes this fan-out only in words.

**Creation prompt:** Create a left-to-right flowchart titled "nlp-ingest-pipeline: chunk, then encode". Node 1 (far left): a document card labeled "Incoming document" containing "title: Moby Dick; Or, The Whale" and "passage_text: long summary text". Arrow to Node 2: a processor box labeled "Processor 1: text_chunking" with parameter lines inside: "algorithm: fixed_token_length", "token_limit: 128", "overlap_rate: 0.2", "tokenizer: standard", and a field mapping note "passage_text -> passage_chunk". Arrow to Node 3: a group of three stacked chunk cards labeled "passage_chunk (3 chunks)": "Chunk 1: tokens 1 to 128", "Chunk 2: tokens 103 to 230", "Chunk 3: tokens 205 to 332". Directly below these cards add an inset strip diagram showing the token stream as one long horizontal bar with three bracket spans above it for the three chunks; shade the two regions where neighboring brackets overlap and label each shaded region "about 26 shared tokens (20% overlap) so phrases that straddle a boundary stay intact in at least one chunk". Arrow from the chunk group to Node 4: a processor box labeled "Processor 2: sparse_encoding" with parameter lines: "model: sparse model (one inference per chunk)", "prune_type: max_ratio", "prune_ratio: 0.1 (drop tokens under 10% of the max weight)", and a field mapping note "passage_chunk -> passage_embedding". Arrow to Node 5 (far right): a document card labeled "Indexed document" showing "passage_text", "passage_chunk", and "passage_embedding: one token-weight map per chunk (nested)". Add a caption under the whole flow: "One long document fans out into several independently searchable chunks; short summaries may stay as one or two." Layout: five stages left to right, the overlap inset sits below stage 3. Style: clean flat technical illustration for a video course slide, white background, limited color palette (blues and grays with one accent color), large readable labels, no logos, no watermark.

## 3. rank_features vs knn_vector: why sparse needs a different field type

**Placement:** Lesson 3-1, Step 5, immediately after the "Why each mapping choice" bullet list and before the "Re-running this chapter?" blockquote.

**Type:** Two-panel comparison graphic with a nested-mapping inset.

**Why:** The chapter says this is the choice that trips people up most, and a picture of the two data shapes plus the nested chunk structure makes the mapping self-evident instead of memorized.

**Creation prompt:** Create a comparison graphic titled "Where each embedding type lives in the index". Two large panels side by side. Left panel titled "knn_vector (dense, Chapter 2)": show a fixed-length horizontal array of small uniform cells with sample values "0.021, -0.114, … , 0.043" and a bracket labeled "always exactly 768 floats". Below it three property lines: "Fixed length, numeric positions", "Every slot filled for every document", "Stored in a k-NN structure (HNSW graph)". Right panel titled "rank_features (sparse, this chapter)": show a variable-length map as key-value rows: "sea: 2.4", "voyage: 2.1", "ship: 1.3", "quest: 0.6", with a bracket labeled "length varies per chunk, string keys". Below it three property lines: "Variable length, token keys are strings", "Only meaningful tokens stored", "Lives in the ordinary inverted index, nearly as cheap as BM25". Between the panels place a small warning callout: "sparse_vector rejects string keys: fails at index time with [sparse_vector] fields should be valid integer". Below both panels add a full-width inset titled "The nested mapping" showing one parent document card "Book: my-sparse-neural-index doc" containing three child cards side by side labeled "Chunk 1 sparse_encoding map", "Chunk 2 sparse_encoding map", "Chunk 3 sparse_encoding map", with a caption: "passage_embedding is type nested: each chunk is an independently queryable sub-document, so a query can score the single best chunk per book (score_mode: max)". Layout: two panels top, warning callout between them, nested inset full width at bottom. Style: clean flat technical illustration for a video course slide, white background, limited color palette (blues and grays with one accent color), large readable labels, no logos, no watermark.

## 4. Three retrieval methods, one query: the ranking bump chart

**Placement:** Lesson 3-1, Step 9, immediately after the three-column comparison table (Lexical, Sparse, Dense) and before the "Three observations" bullet list.

**Type:** Bump chart (slope-style ranking comparison).

**Why:** The table forces students to scan cell by cell for agreements and departures, and a bump chart with connecting lines shows the consensus picks, the arrivals, and the noise at a glance.

**Creation prompt:** Create a bump chart titled "One query, three rankings: 'a hero on a dangerous sea voyage'". Three vertical columns left to right, each listing ranks 1 to 5 top to bottom. Column 1 header "Lexical (BM25)": 1 Kidnapped, 2 Twenty Thousand Leagues under the Sea, 3 Robinson Crusoe, 4 Don Quijote, 5 Treasure Island. Column 2 header "Sparse (neural_sparse)": 1 Twenty Thousand Leagues under the Sea, 2 Treasure Island, 3 Gulliver's Travels, 4 Four Arthurian Romances, 5 Kidnapped. Column 3 header "Dense (neural k-NN)": 1 Gulliver's Travels, 2 Life on the Mississippi, 3 Robinson Crusoe, 4 Treasure Island, 5 Undine. Draw smooth connecting lines between the same book across adjacent columns. Use the accent color and a slightly thicker line for Treasure Island and add a badge on it reading "only book on all three lists". Use a mid-blue for books on exactly two lists (Kidnapped, Twenty Thousand Leagues under the Sea, Robinson Crusoe, Gulliver's Travels). Render single-list books in gray with a small label under each: Don Quijote gets "stopword noise, lexical only", Four Arthurian Romances gets "pure semantic catch, sparse only", Life on the Mississippi and Undine share "thematic drift, dense only". Add a bottom caption: "No single method got it right. Agreement across methods signals true relevance, which is the premise behind fusion." Layout: three columns evenly spaced left to right, lines between them, legend for the three color meanings at the bottom. Style: clean flat technical illustration for a video course slide, white background, limited color palette (blues and grays with one accent color), large readable labels, no logos, no watermark.

## 5. How the hybrid query and normalization pipeline fit together

**Placement:** Lesson 3-2, in the section "Why a search pipeline is required", immediately after the three-bullet list that ends with "trusts the sparse branch more than keyword" and before the Step 1 heading.

**Type:** Architecture and data-flow diagram.

**Why:** Students must understand that two sub-queries run independently and that the processor intervenes after collection but before merging, and that timing is invisible in the request JSON.

**Creation prompt:** Create a top-to-bottom data-flow diagram titled "Hybrid query + normalization pipeline: where the merge happens". Top node: a request box labeled "GET my-sparse-neural-index/_search?search_pipeline=nlp-search-normalization-pipeline" containing a smaller box "hybrid query (up to 5 sub-queries, here 2)". Two arrows fan out to two parallel branch boxes. Left branch titled "Sub-query 0: match (BM25)" with contents "field: passage_text" and a mini result list "raw scores on a 0 to 20 scale: Kidnapped 3.80, Twenty Thousand Leagues 3.03, …". Right branch titled "Sub-query 1: neural_sparse (nested, score_mode: max)" with contents "field: passage_embedding.sparse_encoding" and a mini result list "raw scores on a 0 to 12 scale: Twenty Thousand Leagues 11.75, Treasure Island 11.21, …". Between the two branches place a small crossed-out box: "Naive sum: the larger scale silently wins". Both branches flow down into a wide pipeline band labeled "normalization-processor (phase_results_processors): runs AFTER each branch collects hits, BEFORE OpenSearch merges them". Inside the band show two sequential stages connected by an arrow: Stage 1 "normalization: min_max rescales each branch independently to 0 to 1", Stage 2 "combination: arithmetic_mean with weights [0.3, 0.7]; index 0 is the match branch, index 1 is the sparse branch; weights must sum to 1.0". Add a small warning tag on Stage 2: "weights follow query order; swap the clauses without swapping the weights and you invert your tuning". Bottom node: "One merged ranking, combined scores 0 to 1: Twenty Thousand Leagues 0.91 on top". Layout: single request at top, two parallel branches in the middle, one wide pipeline band below them, single result at the bottom. Style: clean flat technical illustration for a video course slide, white background, limited color palette (blues and grays with one accent color), large readable labels, no logos, no watermark.

## 7. RRF math, worked on the chapter's own results

**Placement:** Lesson 3-3, in the section "What RRF is and why you'd use it", immediately after the paragraph that defines `rank_j` and the rank constant `k`, and before the three-bullet list about `rank_constant`.

**Type:** Worked-math illustration (formula plus calculation table).

**Why:** RRF only clicks when students see real ranks flow through the formula and land exactly on the scores their own cluster returns, and the chapter's numbers verify perfectly.

**Creation prompt:** Create a math explainer graphic titled "RRF: fuse by rank, not by score". At the top show the formula in large type: "RRF score = sum over lists of ( weight_j / ( k + rank_j ) )" with two parameter chips beside it: "k (rank_constant) = 40" and "weights = 0.3 lexical, 0.7 sparse". Below the formula place a four-row worked table with columns "Book", "Lexical rank", "Sparse rank", "Calculation", "RRF score". Row 1: Twenty Thousand Leagues under the Sea, 2, 1, "0.3/(40+2) + 0.7/(40+1) = 0.0071 + 0.0171", 0.0242. Row 2: Treasure Island, 5, 2, "0.3/(40+5) + 0.7/(40+2) = 0.0067 + 0.0167", 0.0233. Row 3: Kidnapped, 1, 5, "0.3/(40+1) + 0.7/(40+5) = 0.0073 + 0.0156", 0.0229. Row 4: Gulliver's Travels, "not in top 5", 3, "0 + 0.7/(40+3)", 0.0163. Highlight the Kidnapped row with the accent color and attach a callout: "Lexical's #1 pick still lands third overall: one strong rank cannot beat agreement across both lists." Add a side note box: "No raw scores appear anywhere in the math, so RRF needs no score normalization and no labeled data to tune. Larger k flattens the differences between ranks; smaller k sharpens them. This chapter uses k = 40 instead of the default 60." Layout: formula at top, table in the middle, callout attached to row 3, side note at bottom right. Style: clean flat technical illustration for a video course slide, white background, limited color palette (blues and grays with one accent color), large readable labels, no logos, no watermark.
